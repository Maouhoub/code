Introduction
Modern single-image super-resolution (SR) methods like SwinIR  (leverage windowed self-attention and
residual blocks) achieve state-of-the-art quality but are computationally heavy. SwinIR’s “lightweight” variant
(e.g. 4 RSTBs × 4 layers) already has fewer parameters , yet further efficiency gains are needed for edge
deployment. Common model compression strategies include architecture design, knowledge distillation,
quantization, or pruning. In fact, recent SR works show that combining techniques can be very effective –
for example, DIPNet uses teacher-student distillation plus iterative L2 filter pruning to speed up RFDN
networks, finding that iterative pruning with L2-norm preserves PSNR much better than one-shot L1
pruning . Similarly, Wu et al. propose compressing SR models by (a) training with sparsity regularization
(L1/OBProx) to find a desired density, then (b) choosing new hyperparameters (channels, layers, blocks) to
match that density, and finally (c) fine-tuning with knowledge distillation from the full model . They
applied this pipeline to SwinIR_lightweight, producing a “SwinIR_mini” with only ~98.8K parameters (vs
878K) while recovering performance via distillation .
Related Work: Pruning & Efficient SR
Efficient SR architectures: Many works design compact SR networks (e.g. IMDN, RFDN) or dynamic
scaling (e.g. multi-scale SR) for efficiency. GhostSR , for instance, shows that CNN-based SR
networks contain redundant “ghost” features and uses shift operations to generate them, reducing
parameters by ~46% with little PSNR loss . However, these focus on CNNs. DIPNet (CVPRW 2023)
explicitly targets SR compression: it uses multi-anchor feature distillation to train a lightweight
student, then iteratively prunes filters with an L2 criterion. In DIPNet, each pruning step removes
5% of filters and is followed by fine-tuning; this iterative L2 pruning yields better PSNR than one-shot
pruning (e.g. ~29.00?dB vs 28.89?dB on DIV2K) . GhostSR, DIPNet, and others underscore that
combining pruning with distillation/reparameterization can preserve quality.
Pruning in Vision Transformers: In parallel, efficient “vision transformer” (ViT/Swin) compression
has gained attention. Zhu et al. (2021) add trainable importance weights for each dimension of the
MHSA and MLP layers, apply L1 sparsity, and prune small dimensions . X-Pruner (2023) introduces
an explainability-aware mask per prunable unit (e.g. attention heads or linear layer channels) and
learns thresholds, demonstrating effective pruning of DeiT and Swin (on ImageNet) with minor
accuracy loss . More recent methods like “Data-independent Module-aware Pruning” use similar
ideas to prune Swin Transformers . Crucially, these works show that structured pruning
(removing entire heads, channels, or dimensions) can greatly reduce computation while being more
hardware-friendly than unstructured (fine-grained) sparsity.
Pruning in SR and Transformers: Applying transformer pruning ideas to SR is less explored. Liang
et al.  (note that SwinIR’s authors) briefly consider a lightweight SwinIR (fewer layers) with
knowledge distillation . A CVPR 2023 paper on dynamic scalable SR (MSSR) applies the Lottery
Ticket Hypothesis: it progressively prunes an SR model to create many nested sparse sub-networks,
using stochastic self-distillation to transfer knowledge from the full model to pruned ones . This

Challenges in Pruning SwinIR
SwinIR’s RSTB blocks contain windowed MSA (multi-head self-attention) and MLP layers. Global
unstructured weight pruning (which the user already tried) often fails for SR because even small weight
drops can severely degrade fine image details. Instead, structured approaches are preferred: e.g., pruning
whole attention heads or MLP channels can remove larger chunks of computation (and is supported by
literature on ViT pruning ). Likewise, pruning entire SwinIR layers or reducing the number of blocks
can cut parameters. However, naively pruning layers hurts PSNR; thus careful importance estimation and
fine-tuning is needed. Another challenge is that SR requires preserving high-frequency texture; so any
pruning must be compensated by restoration (e.g. via distillation on high-frequency features ).
Proposed Contribution: Structured Pruning +
Distillation
A practical, publishable contribution would be to apply layer- and channel-level structured pruning to
SwinIR, combined with light-weight training strategies. Concretely, we propose:
Attention-Head and Channel Pruning: Introduce a small trainable scaling mask for each multihead attention head and for each channel in the MLP layers of SwinIR. For example, each Swin
Transformer layer has H heads; attach a learnable coefficient $a_h$ to the output of head $h$. Apply
an $\ell_1$ regularization on these $a_h$ during training (akin to Zhu et al. ) so that unimportant
heads get pushed toward zero. After training, prune (remove) the heads with coefficients below a
threshold. Similarly, prune neurons/channels in the MLP blocks using sparsity masks. This removes
entire operations and is more hardware-friendly than random weight pruning.
Iterative Pruning & Fine-Tuning: Instead of a one-shot prune, use iterative pruning (like DIPNet) to
minimize quality drop. In each iteration, prune e.g. 10–20% of the least-important heads/channels
(based on the learned masks) and fine-tune the model briefly. This gradual approach, especially with
an $\ell_2$ or $\ell_1$ criterion, tends to preserve PSNR better . After several iterations, one
obtains a compact SwinIR with significantly reduced GFLOPs and parameters.
Knowledge Distillation (KD): To recover lost detail, perform KD from the original SwinIR (or another
high-capacity SR network) to the pruned one. For SR tasks, use both image-space loss and a feature
or pixel distillation loss that emphasizes high-frequency components . For example, minimize $|
I_{\text{SR}}^{\text{pruned}} - I_{\text{SR}}^{\text{teacher}} |^2$ (and a Laplacian-based highfrequency loss as in ). This helps the pruned model learn fine textures that it might otherwise
lose. Prior works (DIPNet, MSSR) have shown distillation can largely restore PSNR after pruning.

Implementation on DIV2K: As requested, evaluate on DIV2K (800 train, 100 validation). Start from
SwinIR_lightweight pretrained weights. During pruning experiments, measure PSNR/SSIM on DIV2K
val and standard benchmarks (Set5, Urban100). Also record inference time or FLOPs for speed. The
goal is a ~10–50% parameter/FLOPs reduction with minimal PSNR drop (e.g. <0.1?dB), which would
be novel and publishable. For example, DIPNet achieved ~30% parameter reduction with negligible
loss . A similar result for a transformer SR model would be significant.
This approach is technically straightforward yet novel in the SR literature. It builds on proven ideas
(structured ViT pruning and SR distillation ) but applies them to SwinIR. Even a small case study
(e.g. pruning 1–2 heads per layer) could yield publishable insights. The minimum contribution is a careful
experimental study: identify which attention heads or channels in SwinIR are redundant for SR, remove
them, and demonstrate that combined pruning+KD outperforms naive pruning. If executed thoroughly
(with ablations, comparisons to CNN baselines, and real SR images), this would merit a Q1/Q2 journal.
Conclusion
In summary, making SwinIR lighter via pruning calls for structured, knowledge-guided methods. State-ofthe-art techniques suggest using channel/ head pruning (not unstructured) and distillation to retain quality
. A concrete, publishable idea is: apply per-head and per-channel sparsity masks in SwinIR’s transformer
layers, prune the smallest units iteratively, and use teacher-student distillation to fine-tune the result. This small
but non-trivial experiment – especially if it yields, say, a 30% parameter cut with <0.1?dB PSNR loss on DIV2K
– would be a meaningful contribution to efficient SR.
